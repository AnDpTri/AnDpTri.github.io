Unsupervised Anomaly Detection and Localization on Industrial Products

Three unsupervised methods (Autoencoder, PaDiM, PatchCore), implemented from scratch and compared under one protocol on 9 product categories from three public datasets, then stress-tested with five follow-up experiments.

Deep Learning course project · My part: Autoencoder (PyTorch), demo application, repository guide; shared protocol and PatchCore runs

At a glance

  • 0.937PatchCore mean image-AUROC over 9 categories
  • 0.969PatchCore mean pixel-AUROC
  • 75%of defective images caught at a 6% false-alarm rate (PatchCore, threshold set on good images)
  • 9categories from MVTec AD, MVTec AD 2 and VisA

The problem

Defective product images are rare, varied and expensive to label, while good images are plentiful. The model learns only from good images and must flag anything that deviates: detect defective images (image-AUROC) and localize the defect pixels (pixel-AUROC and AUPRO).

Methods and data

  • Autoencoder learns to reconstruct images from scratch and uses the reconstruction error as the anomaly signal.
  • PaDiM models normal patch features with a Gaussian per position; PatchCore keeps a memory bank of normal patch features. Both use a pretrained Wide-ResNet-50-2, so the difference between them comes from the mechanism, not the backbone.
  • All three were implemented by the group. Data: grid, screw and transistor (MVTec AD); vial and sheet_metal (MVTec AD 2, with test images under several lighting conditions); pcb1 to pcb4 (VisA).

  • PatchCore: pixel-AUROC 0.969 and AUPRO 0.848. It is the only method that stays usable at a fixed threshold: 75% of defective images caught with 6% false alarms.
  • PaDiM is clearly worse on screw, the only category with free rotation, as expected from tying statistics to position. The Autoencoder is the weakest on MVTec AD but beats PaDiM on the VisA circuit boards (0.925), where a defect changes the colour of a whole region.

Five follow-up experiments

  • Contaminated training set (5–20% defective images): all three methods stay fairly robust; PaDiM is the most robust, PatchCore declines steadily.
  • Few-shot: PatchCore reaches 0.966 AUROC on transistor with only 4 training images. For screw, below about 8 images every method scores under 0.5; the cause is rotation and brightness in the data, not the number of images. Aligning screw orientation helps PaDiM (0.722 to 0.835) and hurts PatchCore (0.955 to 0.915).
  • Model of product A used on product B: not usable. Off the diagonal, AUROC is 0.3–0.7 and every good image of the new product is flagged (false-positive rate 1.00).
  • Lighting and noise shifts: failure modes differ. The Autoencoder fails on brightness changes, PaDiM on noise and position shifts (it collapses on the vial category under over- and under-exposure), PatchCore on blur and very dark images. In many cases AUROC barely changes while the false-alarm rate at the fixed threshold goes to 100%.
  • By defect type: a per-type breakdown with anomaly-map evidence for each failure; see the report.

Limits and how to read this

  • Everything is evaluated on stored images, not on a production line. Thresholds are set on good images only.
  • The Autoencoder results vary more between seeds than the drops measured in some follow-up experiments, so those comparisons are weaker for it.
  • Earlier versions of this project used all 15 MVTec AD categories; the current report replaces them with the 9-category study above, and the two sets of numbers should not be mixed.
